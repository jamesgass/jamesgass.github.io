Dirichlet Characters

This section follows Chapter 2 of Nancy Childress' textbook on Class Field Theory \cite[p.17-40]{childress2008class}.

\begin{Definition}
Let $G$ be a group. A (unitary) character of a group is a homomorphism $\chi: G \to \C^1$. Depending on whether $G$ is written multiplicatively or additively, we say that $\chi$ is a multiplicative or additive character respectively.
\end{Definition}

\begin{Definition}
We say that $\chi: \Z \to \C^1$ is a Dirichlet character (modulo $m$) if there exists $m \in \Z_{\geq 1}$ such that $\chi$ satisfies the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\chi(n+m) = \chi(n) \text{ for all } n \in \Z$;
\item $\chi(kn) = \chi(k)\chi(n) \text{ for all } k,n \in \Z$;
\item $\chi(n) \neq 0$ if and only if $\gcd(m,n) = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Definition}

We can compute some examples of Dirichlet characters in Magma.

\begin{lstlisting}

The multiplication of Dirichlet characters can be defined in the following way. Let $\chi:(\Z/f_\chi\Z)^\times \to \C^1$ and $\psi:(\Z/f_\psi\Z)^\times \to \C^1$ be Dirichlet characters defined on their conductors, and $n = \text{lcm}(f_\chi,f_\psi)$. Then, it is clear that $\mu: (\Z/n\Z)^\times \to \C^1$, given by \[
\mu(a) = \chi(a)\psi(a)
\]
is a (potentially imprimitive) Dirichlet character. Defining the product $\chi\psi$ to be the primitive Dirichlet character which induces $\mu$ then allows us to define a multiplication on primitive Dirichlet characters.

We recall that, if $n | m$, then we have the natural homomorphism $\alpha:(\Z/m\Z)^\times \to (\Z/n\Z)^\times$, $a \mod m \mapsto a \mod n$. If $\chi:(\Z/n\Z)^\times \to \C^1$ is a Dirichlet character modulo $n$, then equally $\chi' = \chi \circ \alpha$ is a Dirichlet character modulo $m$. In this case, $\chi'$ is induced by $\chi$.

\begin{Definition}[The Conductor of a Dirichlet Character]
The conductor $f_\chi$ of a Dirichlet character $\chi$ is the minimal modulus for which it can be defined. If $\chi$ is defined modulo $f_\chi$, then it is said to be primitive.
\end{Definition}

\begin{exercise}
If $(f_\chi, f_\psi) = 1$ then $f_{\chi\psi} = f_\chi f_\psi$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Note that $\lcm(f_\chi,f_\psi) = f_\chi f_\psi$ as $(f_\chi, f_\psi) = 1$. Then, it is clear that $f_{\chi\psi} \mid f_\chi f_\psi$. Now, note that $\chi(n) = \chi\psi(rn - (1-r))$ where $r \equiv 1 \mod{f_\chi}$ and $r \equiv 0 \mod{f_\psi}$. Thus, if $\chi\psi$ is $m$-periodic, the same is true of $\chi$, meaning that its least period divides the conductor of $\chi\psi$. Applying the same argument to $\psi$ allows us to conclude that $f_\chi f_\psi \mid f_{\chi\psi}$ and thus $f_\chi f_\psi = f_{\chi\psi}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{Example}
The above exercise can be observed in Magma in the following way.

\begin{lstlisting}
D5:=FullDirichletGroup(5);
D7 := FullDirichletGroup(7);
D15 := FullDirichletGroup(15);
chi5 := Random(D5);
chi7 := Random(D7);
chi15 := Random(D15);
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}
> chi5;
$.1^3
> chi7;

> chi15;

> Conductor(chi7*chi5);

\end{lstlisting}
\end{Example}

In order to create a (multiplicative) group structure on the (primitive) Dirichlet characters, to every $\chi:(\Z/n\Z)^\times \to \C^1$ we associate $\overline{\chi}:(\Z/n\Z)^\times \to \C^1$, where $\overline{\chi}(n) = \chi(n)^{-1}$. Clearly, $\overline{\chi}$ is a Dirichlet character of the same conductor ($f_{\overline{\chi}} = f_\chi$) and $\chi\overline{\chi} = \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ is the identity character.

Thus, the primitive Dirichlet characters form a multiplicative group. In general, if $G$ is a group, the associated group of characters will be denoted by $\widehat{G}$. There are a couple of notes to make before exploring an analogue of the Galois correspondence using Dirichlet characters.
\begin{itemize}
\item The order of a Dirichlet character is its order within $\widehat{(\Z/n/\Z)^\times}$. Indeed, the order of $\chi \in \widehat{(\Z/n/\Z)^\times}$ is finite as $\text{im}(\chi)$ is a finite group of roots of unity. The order of $\chi$ is a divisor of $\varphi(f_\chi)$.
\item If $\text{ord}(\chi) = 2$, this is often called a quadratic Dirichlet character.
\item For any $\chi \in \widehat{(\Z/n/\Z)^\times}$, given $\chi(-1)^2 = \chi((-1)^2) = \chi(1) = 1$, we must have $\chi(-1) = \pm 1$. In the case where $\chi(-1) = -1$, we say that $\chi$ is odd. Similarly, $\chi$ is even when $\chi(-1) = 1$.

\end{itemize}

\begin{exercise}
Show that $X := \{\chi \in \widehat{(\Z/n/\Z)^\times} : \chi \text{ even}\} \leq \widehat{(\Z/n/\Z)^\times}$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Given $X \subseteq \widehat{(\Z/n/\Z)^\times}$, take $\chi, \psi \in X$ and $m \in (\Z/n \Z)^\times$. Then, $\left(\chi \psi^{-1}\right)(m) = \chi(m)\overline{\psi(m)}$. Letting $m \equiv -1 \mod{n}$, $\chi(-1)\overline{\psi(-1)} = (1) \overline{(1)} = 1$, meaning that $\chi\psi^{-1} \in X$ and hence $X \leq \widehat{(\Z/n/\Z)^\times}$.
\end{solution}

Galois Correspondence

Given the isomorphism of the groups $(\Z / n \Z)^\times \cong \Gal(\Q(\zeta_n) | \Q)$, we can identify the Dirichlet characters $\widehat{(\Z / n \Z)^\times}$ with $\widehat{\Gal(\Q(\zeta_n) | \Q)}$. As such, for the rest of this section we will denote the group of Dirichlet characters as $\widehat{G}$, where $G$ refers to both $(\Z/n\Z)^\times$ and $\Gal(\Q(\zeta_n) | \Q)$.

\begin{Definition}[Field of Moduli of a Dirichlet Character]\label{def:dirichlet_field_of_moduli}
Given a Dirichlet character $\chi: G \to \C^1$, we say that $K_\chi := \Q(\zeta_n)^{\ker(\chi)}$ is the field of moduli of $\chi$ (or the field associated with $\chi$).
\end{Definition}

\begin{Remark}
Equivalently, Definition \ref{def:dirichlet_field_of_moduli} can be thought of in the following way. Let $H \leq G$ be the subgroup of all automorphisms $\sigma \in G$ satisfying $\chi(\sigma\tau) = \chi(\tau)$ for all $\tau \in G$. Then, $K_\chi = \Q(\zeta_n)^H$.
\end{Remark}

\begin{Example}
Here, we let $\chi \in$ \lstinline|FullDirichletGroup(15)| be as the previous example. We see that:

\begin{lstlisting}
for n in [0..14] do
print "n =", n;
print "chi15(n) =", chi15(n);
end for;
\end{lstlisting}

yields $\chi(n) =1$ for $n \equiv 1,2,4,8 \in (\Z / 15 \Z)^\times$. Then,

\begin{lstlisting}
K := CyclotomicField(15);
H_auts := [ hom< K -> K | z^k > : k in [1, 2, 4, 8] ];
FixedField(K, H_auts);
\end{lstlisting}

tells us that $K_\chi = \Q(\zeta_{15})^{\{id, \sigma_2, \sigma_4, \sigma_8\}} = \Q(\sqrt{-15})$.

\end{Example}

\begin{Example}
In this example, we consider the characters over $\Q(\zeta_n)$ and $X \subseteq \widehat{G}$ the subset of even characters. A previous exercise showed that this is also a subgroup. Indeed, $\sigma_{-1} \in G$ acts as complex conjugation, and $\chi(\sigma_{-1}) = \chi(-1) = 1$ should indicate that the field $K$ associated to $X$ is real.

The previous observation indicates that $\{\text{id}, \sigma_{-1}\} \subseteq \bigcap_{\chi \in X}\ker \chi$. Assuming for now that the opposite inclusion is true, we compute some of the associated fields to $X$.

\begin{lstlisting}
for n in [3, 5, 9, 14] do
print "n =", n;
K := CyclotomicField(n);
H_auts := [ hom< K -> K | z^k > : k in [1,n-1] ];
K:=FixedField(K, H_auts);
K;
Signature(K);
end for;
\end{lstlisting}

Here, \lstinline|Signature(K)| refers to the real and complex embeddings. The output indicates
\begin{lstlisting}
n = 3

n = 5
Number Field with defining polynomial $.1^2 - $.1 - 1 over the Rational Field
2 0
n = 9
Number Field with defining polynomial $.1^3 - 3*$.1^2 + 1 over the Rational Field
3 0
n = 14
Number Field with defining polynomial $.1^3 - 4*$.1^2 + 3*$.1 + 1 over the Rational Field
3 0
\end{lstlisting}
that each of the fields are indeed real.

We will now show that $ \bigcap_{\chi \in X}\ker \chi \subseteq \{\text{id}, \sigma_{-1}\}$. Take $\sigma_r \in \bigcap_{\chi \in X}\ker \chi$. Under the canonical isomorphism $G \to \widehat{\widehat{G}}$ \footnote{Assuming that $\Psi:G \to \widehat{\widehat{G}}$, given by $x \mapsto \Tilde{x}$ where $\Tilde{x}: \widehat{G} \to \C^1$ maps $\chi \overset{\Tilde{x}}{\mapsto}\chi(x)$, is a homomorphism, we demonstrate injectivity by first supposing that there is a $g \in G$ satisfyng $\chi(g) = 1$ for all $\chi \in \widehat{G}$. Then, we may view the distinct characters of $\widehat{G}$ as those of $G / \langle g \rangle$. Then, there are $\# G / \langle g \rangle$ such characters. This tells us that $\# \langle g \rangle = 1$ and the only such $g \in G$ is $g=1$. Thus, $\Psi$ is injective and given $\#G = \#\widehat{\widehat{G}}$, $\Psi$ must also be an isomorphism.}.
\end{Example}
